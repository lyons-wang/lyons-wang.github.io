Neural Representation of Dance

Final Project for NEUR1660: Neural Computation in Learning and Decision Making

Abstract:

This project explores how reinforcement learning can model aspects of human dance learning. Using Q-learning, we trained an agent to learn a fixed choreography sequence through trial and error, integrating rhythm, movement memory, and feedback. We compared multiple reward structures and state representations to investigate how sequencing, timing, and intermediate feedback influence the learning of complex dance patterns.

Key Results:

  • Chunked/curriculum learning dramatically outperformed sparse reward learning for choreography acquisition
  • Intermediate feedback was critical for learning long movement sequences effectively
  • Models with explicit sequence-position memory consistently outperformed rhythm-only state representations

To summarize: these results suggest that both reward structure and state representation strongly influence an agents' ability to learn sequential choreography. While reinforcement learning was effective for reproducing fixed movement patterns, the project also highlighted important limitations in modeling the creativity, flexibility, and embodiment involved in real human dance learning.

Above: Comparison of the two strongest learning approaches. Both memorization and chunked/curriculum learning achieved full-sequence mastery, though curriculum learning progressed more gradually through smaller movement sections.

Go AI Agent

Final Project for CSCI0410: Foundations of AI & Machine Learning

Abstract:

This project explored artificial intelligence strategies for playing 5x5 Go under strict time constraints. I implemented and compared multiple search-based agents, including Minimax, Alpha-Beta Pruning, Iterative Deepening Search (IDS), and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), before developing a hybrid agent that combined adversarial search with probabilistic exploration. The project investigated how different search strategies balance computational efficiency, board evaluation quality, and decision-making under limited time budgets.

Hybrid Agent Design

The final agent used a hybrid strategy that adapted to different stages of the game. During the opening, the agent prioritized center control to establish strong early influence while avoiding unnecessary search during the highest-branching portion of the game. This opening strategy was inspired by 5x5 Go analysis from Sensei's Library.

In the early and middle game, the agent relied on Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), which explored possible outcomes through probabilistic simulations rather than depending entirely on heuristic evaluation. In the late game, when fewer legal actions remained (< 10), the agent switched to Iterative Deepening Search (IDS), allowing deeper tactical evaluation of more developed board states.

This hybrid design combined the strengths of both approaches: broad exploration during complex early-game positions and more precise more precise heuristic-guided evaluation during the late game.

Heuristic Design

The heuristic evaluation function estimated board strength using both stone count and controlled territory. Existing stones on the board were weighted heavily because they represented secured points and stable board presence.

To evaluate territory, the agent searched for connected empty regions using a flood-fill algorithm. The algorithm iterated across the board and expanded through neighboring empty intersections to identify contiguous empty regions. During the search, the agent tracked which player's stones bordered each region. If a region was completely surrounded by only one player, the region was counted as controlled territory and added to that player's score. This territory evaluation strategy was inspired by traditional Go scoring principles described by the Pandanet Go Guide .

The final heuristic combined weighted stone count and territorial control into a single evaluation score representing overall board strength. Positive scores favored black, while negative scores favored white. Territorial regions were weighted less heavily than placed stones because territory can still be invaded or reduced later in the game.

Results:

The hybrid agent performed competitively under tournament evaluation by combining probabilistic exploration during the early game with deeper heuristic-guided evaluation in the late game. Iterative Deepening Search consistently outperformed standard Monte Carlo Tree Search in many local tests, largely because heuristic evaluation was more computationally efficient than repeated Monte Carlo rollouts under strict time constraints.

Interestingly, the Greedy agent also performed competitively against several stronger search-based agents. On a small 5x5 board, immediate positional advantages and local captures can already provide strong short-term evaluations, allowing simpler heuristic-based decision making to remain surprisingly effective despite limited lookahead.

Although the hybrid model sometimes underperformed locally due to rollout speed limitations, it achieved the strongest overall performance on the Gradescope autograder evaluation. This suggests that the hybrid approach was able to balance exploration and tactical evaluation effectively under the official testing environment and time controls.

One major limitation of the project was runtime performance. Because the implementation used a PyGame/PyGo-based environment, Monte Carlo rollouts were relatively slow, causing MCTS to occasionally exceed time limits and forfeit games. As a result, some tournament outcomes likely reflected both strategic strength and computational efficiency rather than pure gameplay quality.

Figure 1: Tournament win-rate heatmap comparing implemented agents across local matchups. In this small local sample, the Greedy agent performed surprisingly well, likely because immediate positional advantages are especially valuable on a 5x5 board. MCTS performed poorly across matchups, likely due to slow rollout computation and time-limit issues.

Experiments with different MCTS exploration constants also demonstrated how exploration-exploitation tradeoffs influenced search behavior and move selection. Lower exploration constants caused the search to focus heavily on a smaller number of promising actions, while higher values distributed simulations more broadly across the board.

Figure 2: Distribution of MCTS visits across actions for different exploration constants. Lower values of c encouraged exploitation, while larger values increased exploration across available actions.

Future Work:

One possible extension would terminate flood-fill searches early when mixed-color borders are detected, avoiding unnecessary exploration of regions that cannot contribute to territory scoring.

Privacy in the Healthcare System

Final Project for CSCI1805: Computers, Freedom, and Privacy

Abstract:

This project examined how modern digital health technologies have outpaced traditional healthcare privacy regulations such as HIPAA. Through analysis of legal frameworks, cybersecurity risks, and ethical implications, the project explored how wearable devices, telehealth platforms, and third-party health applications create new challenges for patient privacy in the digital age.

Core Challenges

  • HIPAA does not regulate many modern digital health platforms
  • Healthcare data breaches have sharply increased in the digital era
  • Third-party apps and wearables collect sensitive patient data with limited oversight
  • Fragmented state and federal laws create regulatory complexity

Ethical Concerns

  • Risk of re-identification from “anonymous” datasets
  • Commercial exploitation of patient data
  • Threats to patient autonomy and confidentiality
  • Disproportionate harm to vulnerable populations

Policy Recommendations

  • Expand FTC oversight of digital health technologies
  • Establish national privacy and cybersecurity standards
  • Limit third-party data sharing and commercialization
  • Create certification and accountability frameworks

Neural Signal Processing & Connectivity Analysis

Final Exam for NEUR2110: Statistical Neuroscience

Abstract:

This project explored a range of computational neuroscience methods for analyzing neural signals, connectivity, and dynamical systems. Using stochastic process analysis, spectral estimation, Granger causality, generalized linear models (GLMs), and Kalman filtering, the project investigated how statistical and dynamical modeling techniques can be used to characterize neural activity across both continuous and spike-train data.

Analyses included spectral coherence estimation, effective connectivity inference, point-process modeling of neuronal spiking, and latent state estimation in Gaussian linear dynamical systems. Together, these methods demonstrate how modern computational approaches can uncover structure, information flow, and temporal dynamics in complex neural systems.
